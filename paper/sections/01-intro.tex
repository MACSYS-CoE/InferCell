\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A whole-cell model simulates a cell from its molecular parts. The first such model
described \textit{Mycoplasma genitalium} with \pn{lit.karrsub} coupled submodels
\cite{karr2012whole}. Later models covered \textit{Escherichia coli}
\cite{macklin2020simultaneous} and the minimal cell \syn{} \cite{hutchison2016design}. After
models of its metabolism \cite{breuer2019essential} and its genetic information processes
\cite{thornburg2019kinetic}, \syn{} was modelled whole, first well stirred
\cite{thornburg2022fundamental} and then in space and time over a full cell cycle
\cite{thornburg2026bringing}. Each of these whole-cell models joins
metabolism, gene expression, growth and division in one simulation, and each
reproduces measured behaviour of a real cell.

All of these models are forward simulators. Each one takes hundreds or thousands of kinetic
parameters from the literature, sets each to one value, and integrates forward in
time. The output is one trajectory. That trajectory is one sample from the
distribution of trajectories that the parameter knowledge allows. The model does not
know the width of that distribution. When a model predicts a doubling time, a reader
cannot tell whether the prediction holds for every plausible parameter set or depends on a few uncertain values. Sensitivity analyses vary parameters \cite{macklin2020simultaneous}, but they do not say what the data support.

The missing error bar matters because every parameter in a whole-cell model is
uncertain. A rate measured in one organism, at one temperature,
in one medium, is then used in another organism under other conditions. That
uncertainty propagates through coupled nonlinear dynamics into every prediction. A model
that stores each parameter as one number cannot ask how uncertain a prediction is, or
which data would reduce that uncertainty.

\subsection{Why whole-cell models do not do inference}

Bayesian inference answers both questions: how uncertain a prediction is, and which data
would reduce that uncertainty. It treats each parameter as a
distribution, updates the distribution with data, and propagates the result into
predictions. This approach has been argued for whole-cell models \cite{babtie2017how}.
Whole-cell parameters have been estimated, but as point estimates and not as calibrated
posteriors. In the DREAM8 challenge, teams recovered \pn{dream.nparam} parameters of a
modified \textit{M.\ genitalium} model, \pn{dream.nmod} changed and \pn{dream.nmod} unchanged,
scored against that one known truth \cite{karr2015summary}. Parameter error and prediction
error had a log--log $R^2$ of \pn{dream.rall} over all submissions and only \pn{dream.rtop}
among the top solutions. The authors note that several parameter sets give similar
system-level phenotypes. A single estimate cannot express that degeneracy, and a posterior can
\cite{gutenkunst2007universally}. No team used automatic differentiation, and the authors ask for automated reduced models and for data on single-cell variation over
time. Apart from Bayesian balancing of metabolic parameters \cite{lubitz2010parameter,thornburg2022fundamental}, we know of
no whole-cell model
that reports posteriors over its parameters. We see five reasons.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{No place for uncertainty.} The frameworks store parameters as
  floating-point values in a dictionary. The source of each value is in a
  spreadsheet, a flat file or a supplement, and rarely with the value in the model.
  \item \emph{Mixed formalisms.} Metabolism is a set of ordinary differential
  equations (ODEs). Gene expression is a stochastic jump process. The two exchange
  state on a fixed clock. Exact samplers for such hybrids exist, such as particle Markov chain
  Monte Carlo \cite{andrieu2010particle,golightly2011bayesian}, but none is standard practice
  at whole-cell scale.
  \item \emph{Latent paths.} The stochastic block has a hidden path: the exact
  times of each transcription and translation event. The likelihood of the data
  integrates over all such paths, and that integral has no closed form.
  \item \emph{Cost.} Inference needs many simulations, and the cost of a posterior is
  their number times the time of one. A route that needs fewer simulations can matter as much as a
  faster simulator.
  \item \emph{No shared parameters.} In the published \syn{} model, no rate
  constant appears in both blocks. The blocks couple only through state. The
  standard methods for modular inference pass information through shared
  parameters \cite{plummer2015cuts,jacob2017better,carmona2020semimodular}.
  Here those methods do not apply directly.
\end{enumerate}

\Cref{tab:frameworks} compares current whole-cell frameworks on four design features.
Thornburg and coworkers balanced the metabolic parameters of \syn{} against each
other with a Bayesian method, parameter balancing \cite{lubitz2010parameter,thornburg2022fundamental}. That step used
literature values for metabolism only. It did not infer parameters from dynamic
data through the coupled model.

Outside whole-cell modelling, Bayesian inference for stochastic kinetic models is well developed
\cite{wilkinson2018stochastic}. Pseudo-marginal and particle Markov chain Monte Carlo methods
replace an intractable likelihood with an unbiased estimate of it
\cite{andrieu2009pseudomarginal,andrieu2010particle,doucet2015efficient}, and have been applied to
stochastic biochemical networks \cite{golightly2011bayesian}. When a model is built from modules,
cutting feedback stops one module's data from changing another module's posterior
\cite{liu2009modularization,plummer2015cuts,jacob2017better}. Recent work extends cuts to
variational, likelihood-free and generalised Bayesian inference
\cite{yu2023variational,chakraborty2023modularized,frazier2025cutting,liu2025general}, and Markov
melding joins chains of submodels that share quantities \cite{manderson2023combining}.
\Cref{sec:discussion} compares other approximate routes to a posterior.

What is missing is a whole-cell model inferred from single-cell and bulk data across a real
ODE--stochastic boundary, with its posterior predictions scored against a known truth.
Without one, we cannot say whether such a posterior is affordable, or whether the data reach
the parameters on both sides of the boundary. Nor can we say how far its predictions carry
past the data that made it.

\subsection{This work}

We present \IC{}, a compositional inference framework for whole-cell models. We use it
to infer a reduced whole-cell model of \syn{}, with a likelihood that spans its ODE--stochastic
boundary, and to carry the posterior to scored probabilistic predictions.
\Cref{sec:design} describes the framework. Every parameter is a prior with a recorded
source, modules couple through \pn{fw.edgekinds} kinds of typed edge, and every departure
from the published model is a declared reduction. The composed model runs either as the
exact hybrid, with gene expression as a stochastic jump process, or with gene expression
swapped for mean-field ODEs. The two descriptions give two inference
routes: an exact sampler that conditions on the stochastic path, and a mixed route that
integrates it out.

\Cref{sec:model} sets up the test system. The reduced \syn{} model keeps
\pn{model.rxn} metabolic reactions, \pn{model.genes} genes and the two-way coupling between
them. Its synthetic single-cell and bulk data come from a known truth. M0, a two-gene
slice of it, is small enough for the exact sampler. There, \cref{sec:posteriors} shows that
both routes pass simulation-based calibration, a test that at this size detects only large
miscalibration, and that the mixed route costs about \pn{mix.speedup} times less than an exact
production chain. On one dataset the two routes still differ by
\pn{mix.gaplo}--\pn{mix.gaphi} standard deviations, for a cause we have not found. \Cref{sec:reduced} then uses the mixed route
alone to infer one joint posterior over the \pn{kim92.ncoord} free parameters of the
reduced model, for about \pn{kim92.cpuh} CPU-hours. The data reach all \pn{kim92.ncoord},
on both sides of the boundary, and the posterior reproduces its own data. The promoter
strengths sit about \pn{kim92.promshift}\% low, for a cause we have not found.
Neither of two
two-stage alternatives, as designed, is both cheaper and faithful. A factor cut lands close to
the joint posterior at \pn{kim116.cutt1} times its cost, and a cheaper data plug-in shifts the
promoters away from it and predicts lactate worse.

\Cref{sec:reducedpredict} pushes that
posterior forward to a later horizon and to a ptsG knockdown, neither of which the fit saw.
It scores the predictions against the true simulator. They hold the truth for extracellular
lactate at the end of the cycle, at baseline and under the knockdown, and for the transcripts.
They miss \pn{kim95.poolshift} of the \pn{mzero.npanel} metabolite pools, though not the
extracellular lactate pool, and they do not resolve the
knockdown's effect on lactate, which is small next to cell noise. With every parameter inferred, the lactate
prediction's parameter variance is about \pn{kim96.ratio} times smaller (\pn{kim96.ratiolo}--\pn{kim96.ratiohi}
at $\pm 2$ standard errors) than in the budget transferred from M0. That budget carries an M0
posterior, fitted to a different dataset, to the reduced model and leaves the other promoters
at their priors. The factor holds at the default prior width; over widths $\tfrac12$ to 2 it
runs from \pn{kim96.widthlo} to \pn{kim96.widthhi}.
\Cref{sec:discussion} sets out what carries, what does not, and the limits of the test.

The claim has limits. The data are synthetic, they come from the model being fitted, and
the reduced model has one dataset and one truth. Only the promoter strengths, the
transcript decay rate, two catalytic constants and the bulk assay noise are free.
Simulation-based calibration runs on M0 only, and we make no claim about \syn{} itself.
